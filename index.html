Rover wheel

A wheel for a small rover, designed in SolidWorks and 3D printed. The edge is cut with fine teeth to grip the ground, and ten triangular cutouts keep the wheel light. I compared PLA, PETG, and TPU to choose a material and thought through print orientation so the part prints strong.

The first version needed hot glue to hold the axle. I'm redesigning the axle hole so the wheel stays on with a friction fit instead.

Alexa voice assistant powered by Claude

I turned an Echo Dot into a voice assistant powered by Claude, using Claude to help write the code. An Alexa skill sends what you say to AWS Lambda, which asks Anthropic's Claude for an answer and sends it back to be spoken aloud.

I set up the Alexa skill and the AWS Lambda function, got API access through Pitt's AI Enablement team, and worked through each error with Claude's help until the whole chain ran reliably.

What went wrong

At first Alexa kept saying there was a problem with the skill's response. I raised the Lambda timeout from 3 seconds to 30, which didn't help. I then checked the logs, which showed the real cause: a syntax error from a partly updated function, a try block with no matching except.

After that, Alexa answered with its own “Sorry, I don't know that one,” so my skill wasn't matching what I said. With Claude's help, I fixed it by adjusting the sample phrases and how unmatched requests were routed. Claude's early answers also had markdown asterisks that a speaker would read aloud, so I added instructions telling it to answer in short, plain sentences.

The finished assistant resolved most of these problems, but one limitation remains. I still have to say “Hey Alexa” before I say anything, which rules out a natural, back-and-forth conversation with Claude.

Skyblock Bazaar tracker

Type
Independent project
Tools
Python, PyWebView, SQLite, Anthropic API, PyInstaller

Hypixel Skyblock is a Minecraft minigame that I play for fun in my free time. It has a large player-driven economy, and the Bazaar is its main market for raw materials and everyday items.

The Bazaar works a lot like a small stock market. Players post buy orders and sell offers at the prices they want and wait for someone to fill them, or they use instant buy and instant sell to trade right away at the best price currently available. Instant trades are fast but give up some profit, while orders usually get a better price but can take hours or days to fill. Players make coins from the gap between the buy and sell prices, a strategy called flipping.

I was curious how AI could help me use the Bazaar more effectively, so I built a tracker with help from Claude and the Claude API. It checks the Bazaar's public data every 60 seconds and saves each snapshot to a local SQLite database, keeping 30 days of price history.

The app has four tabs. Flip Finder ranks items with a score that weighs sellable volume, profit margin, and total profit. Craft Flips compares buying ingredients against selling the crafted item after the 1.25% sell tax. Investments scores demand, supply, and the 24-hour price trend. Item Advisor shows the live order book and price history, then asks Claude whether to buy, sell, wait, or avoid.

It started as a web page, but the browser blocked the data requests. With Claude's help, I moved it to a Python desktop app that makes those requests itself and packaged it with PyInstaller into a program that runs without a terminal. Getting that version working meant testing it, reporting each error back to Claude, and checking the fixes, including bugs that only appeared in the packaged build, like settings and the database saving to a temporary folder.

3D printing in automotive engineering

Event
Pitt First Year Engineering Conference
Format
Team presentation

I presented with two teammates at the University of Pittsburgh's First Year Engineering Conference. We researched how automakers such as Volkswagen, Ford, and BMW use 3D printing throughout the process of designing and building a car.

We started with the history of 3D printing in the automotive field. At first it was mostly a way to make prototypes and one-off parts quickly, so engineers could test a design before paying for expensive tooling. Over time it became a dependable production tool, and today it is used for the jigs, fixtures, and other tools that assembly lines run on, as well as some parts that go into the cars themselves.

From there we looked at how automakers use it today. That included the tools and fixtures that 3D printing lets factories make quickly and cheaply. I enjoyed answering questions afterward from engineers and researchers who work on similar problems who attended our presentation.
